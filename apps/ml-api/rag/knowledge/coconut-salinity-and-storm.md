# Coconut management under salinity and storms

## Coconut and salt

Coconut is one of the more salt-tolerant tree crops and grows naturally along coasts. It is generally rated as moderately tolerant of salinity and can survive brief sea water flooding if the water drains within a few days. Young seedlings are more sensitive than mature palms. Long-term salinity and waterlogging together still reduce yields.

Coconut also benefits from **chloride**, which improves nut production and water balance. In inland areas far from the sea, some advisories (for example from CPCRI in India) recommend applying **common salt (sodium chloride) at about 1–2 kg per palm per year** in the basin, especially on laterite or sandy soils low in chloride. This is not needed in coastal areas already exposed to salt spray or saline groundwater, and it must never be applied where soil is already saline.

## Fertilizer for mature palms

A typical recommendation for adult palms in India is roughly:
- 500 g N, 320 g P2O5 and 1,200 g K2O per palm per year, applied in 2 splits (start and end of monsoon), plus 20–50 kg of organic manure per palm.
- Apply in a circular basin about 1.8–2 m around the trunk and mix into the soil.
- **Apply** extra potassium (muriate of potash) in saline soils, as it helps palms tolerate salt stress.

## After storm surge

1. **Drain** saline water from the plantation as quickly as possible by opening channels.
2. **Flush** palm basins with fresh water or rain water where available.
3. Loosen basin soil and add organic matter and mulch (husks or fronds).
4. Monitor for yellowing of lower fronds; mature palms often recover over 1–2 years.

## After cyclones and strong winds

1. **Remove** broken and hanging fronds carefully so they do not tear the crown.
2. Palms with a **broken or snapped crown will not recover**; cut them down and use the trunk as lumber.
3. For palms leaning but with an intact crown and roots, straighten young palms and prop them with poles; old palms leaning heavily usually recover by growing upward.
4. **Protect the growing bud:** apply Bordeaux paste or another recommended copper fungicide to damaged crowns to prevent bud rot, which often follows storm damage and heavy rain.
5. Clean up fallen trunks and debris quickly; rotting palms breed rhinoceros beetle and red palm weevil.
6. Apply a balanced fertilizer and organic manure to help recovery.

## Replanting

1. **Replant** at the start of the rainy season with healthy seedlings about 9–12 months old with 6 or more leaves.
2. Use spacing of about 7.5 x 7.5 m (roughly 175 palms per hectare) in square planting.
3. Dig pits about 1 x 1 x 1 m, fill with topsoil, organic manure and a little husk.
4. Shade young seedlings in the first dry season and irrigate with fresh water.
5. Choose dwarf or hybrid varieties with shorter stature where recommended, as they are less exposed to wind; tall varieties are often more wind-tolerant once established, so follow local advice.

## Intercropping

Mixed cropping spreads risk and uses space between palms:
- In young plantations: banana, pineapple, vegetables, ginger, turmeric.
- In mature plantations: cocoa, black pepper on palm trunks, fodder grass, pineapple.
- In salt-affected areas choose more tolerant intercrops and avoid sensitive vegetables.

## Insurance and support

Coconut damage from cyclones is covered by some state and national schemes. Record the number of damaged palms with photos and report to the agriculture or horticulture officer or coconut authority promptly.
