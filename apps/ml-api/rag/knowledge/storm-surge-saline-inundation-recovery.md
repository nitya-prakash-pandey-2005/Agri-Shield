# Recovering farmland after storm surge and saline inundation

## What sea water does to fields

Storm surge brings sea water with an EC of roughly 45–55 dS/m onto fields. Even after the water drains, salt stays in the soil. Sodium can also damage soil structure, making it sticky when wet and hard when dry. Standing crops are usually killed within days. After Cyclone Aila (2009) in south-west Bangladesh, some breached polders stayed saline for about two years because embankments were not repaired quickly.

## Step 1 — Get the salt water out (first days)

1. **Close breaches** in embankments and bunds first; otherwise each high tide brings more salt water.
2. **Drain** surge water as fast as possible through outlets and **open sluice gates at low tide**.
3. Do not let saline water stand and evaporate on fields; evaporation leaves salt behind.
4. Remove sand and marine debris deposits thicker than a few centimetres before ploughing; mixing thick sand layers into the soil reduces fertility.

## Step 2 — Measure salinity

1. **Measure** soil EC (1:5 soil:water) at 0–15 cm and 15–30 cm depth in several spots per field.
2. Convert to ECe (multiply roughly by 6–9 for clay and clay-loam soils; more for sandy soils).
3. Measure EC of canal and pond water you plan to use for flushing.
4. Repeat every 2–4 weeks to track progress.

## Step 3 — Leach the salts

1. **Flush** with fresh water: pond 5–10 cm of rain or fresh canal water (EC below about 2 dS/m), leave 1–2 days, **drain**, and repeat.
2. Monsoon rain is the cheapest flushing water. Keep drains open so rain water carries salt away rather than just sitting on the field.
3. Several flushes are usually needed. Roughly, ponding and draining about 30 cm of fresh water in total removes a large part of the salt from the top 30 cm of soil if drainage works.

## Step 4 — Treat sodium and rebuild soil

1. Where a soil test shows high sodium (high ESP or pH above about 8.5 after surge), **apply gypsum** before leaching. Rates depend on the test; from several hundred kg/ha for mild cases up to a few t/ha for sodic soils.
2. **Add organic matter**: farmyard manure or compost at about 5–10 t/ha, or grow a green manure such as dhaincha where salinity allows.
3. **Apply** extra potassium and zinc sulphate (for example about 10 kg/ha) in the next crop, because sodium reduces their uptake.
4. **Mulch** bare soil with straw (about 3–5 t/ha) in the dry season to reduce salt rising to the surface.

## Step 5 — Choose the next crop

| Soil ECe after leaching | Suggested options |
|---|---|
| below about 3 dS/m | Normal rice and most crops |
| about 3–6 dS/m | Salt-tolerant rice varieties; barley, sunflower, cotton, sugar beet in dry season |
| about 6–8 dS/m | Salt-tolerant rice with care; tolerant crops only |
| above about 8 dS/m | Continue leaching; fallow or aquaculture this season |

- **Transplant** older rice seedlings (about 30–35 days), 3 per hill.
- Grow vegetables on raised beds or in homestead gardens with fresh water.

## Step 6 — Document and seek support

1. Take dated photos of saline water on fields, dead crops and salt crusts.
2. **Report** to the local agriculture officer and insurer within your scheme's deadline.
3. Ask for salt-tolerant seed, gypsum and input support available after declared disasters.

## Trees and perennial crops

Coconut is moderately tolerant and often survives one surge if drained quickly. Banana, papaya, mango and most vegetables are sensitive. Flush the root zone of valuable trees with fresh water where possible.
