# Community sluice gate management in polders and embankments

## Why sluice gates matter

Coastal polders and embankments in Bangladesh, the Mekong Delta, the Odisha coast and the Java north coast depend on sluice gates to let fresh water in, keep salt water out and drain excess rain. A single gate opened at the wrong time can let saline water into hundreds of hectares. Good operation is a community responsibility.

## Basic operating rules

### Dry season (salinity risk)
1. **Close sluice gates during high tide** to keep saline river water out.
2. Open gates to take in water only when the river EC is below the agreed safe level (for rice, about 2 dS/m) — usually at low tide or when upstream fresh flow is strong.
3. **Measure** river and canal EC daily near the gate during the dry season, and before any opening.
4. Keep internal canals full of stored fresh water; do not drain them unnecessarily.

### Wet season (flood and drainage risk)
1. **Open gates at low tide to drain** excess rain water from the polder.
2. Close gates as the tide rises so river water does not flow back in.
3. Before a forecast storm or cyclone, **drain internal canals at low tide** to create storage space for heavy rain.
4. During storm surges, keep gates closed and check them for leaks.

## Community organisation

- **Bangladesh:** Water Management Organisations (Water Management Groups and Associations) formed under BWDB's Guidelines for Participatory Water Management are responsible for operating and maintaining small gates in their polder. Programmes such as Blue Gold have supported these groups in Khulna, Satkhira and Patuakhali.
- **Vietnam:** provincial irrigation companies and commune authorities operate main sluices; farmers coordinate through commune people's committees and cooperatives. Large systems (for example in Ca Mau peninsula and Ben Tre) are operated according to provincial salinity monitoring.
- **India and Indonesia:** sluice or flap gates on coastal embankments are usually managed by the state water resources or irrigation department, with village committees helping locally.

## Setting up a gate operation plan

1. Form a gate committee with farmers from upper and lower parts of the polder, including rice farmers, shrimp farmers and women farmers.
2. Agree written rules: who opens and closes the gate, safe EC levels, drainage priorities and emergency contacts.
3. Keep a gate logbook: date, time, tide, EC reading, gate open or closed, and who operated it.
4. Share tide tables and salinity bulletins with all members, for example through a phone group.
5. Resolve conflicts between shrimp farmers who want saline water and rice farmers who need fresh water through agreed zoning and timing.

## Maintenance

- Before the dry season and before the monsoon, **clean the gate** and remove silt and water hyacinth from the gate channel.
- Grease the lifting mechanism, check the door seals and repair leaks.
- Re-excavate silted internal canals so drainage works.
- Report damaged gates or embankment cracks to the water development or irrigation office immediately.

## After a cyclone or embankment breach

1. Close all working gates to stop further tidal inflow.
2. Report breaches to the authorities and organise community closing of small breaches with sandbags where safe.
3. Once the breach is closed, **drain saline water at low tide** through the gates as quickly as possible.
4. Monitor soil and water EC before replanting.
