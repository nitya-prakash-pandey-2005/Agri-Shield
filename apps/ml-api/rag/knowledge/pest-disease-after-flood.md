# Rice pests and diseases after floods — scouting and IPM

## Why problems increase after floods

Flood water spreads bacteria and fungal spores, wounds leaves, and leaves plants weak. Rats crowd onto bunds and surviving fields. Replanted crops are often out of step with neighbours, which helps pests build up. Careful scouting and integrated pest management (IPM) prevent a second loss.

## Scouting schedule

1. Walk the field **every 5–7 days** for the first 4 weeks after the water recedes, then weekly.
2. Check at least 10–20 hills in a zigzag pattern across the field.
3. Look at leaves, stems at the water line, and the base of the hills.
4. Record what you see and the date.

## Bacterial leaf blight (BLB)

- **Signs:** wavy yellow to straw-coloured stripes starting from leaf tips and edges; in young plants, leaves wilt and roll (kresek). Bacterial ooze drops may be seen in the morning.
- **Spread:** by flood and irrigation water, wind and rain, and through wounds.
- **Actions:**
  1. **Drain** the field for 3–4 days and keep water shallow.
  2. Do not apply extra nitrogen; **apply potassium** (for example about 20–30 kg K2O/ha).
  3. Remove weeds and volunteer rice that host the bacteria.
  4. Use resistant varieties in the next season.
  5. Chemical control is generally not effective or economical.

## Sheath blight

- **Signs:** oval grey-green lesions with brown borders on sheaths near the water line, spreading upward in dense, lush crops.
- **Actions:** avoid excess nitrogen, keep proper spacing, drain periodically. If more than about 20–25% of tillers are infected at maximum tillering to booting, apply a recommended fungicide at label rate.

## Brown spot

- **Signs:** oval brown spots with grey centres on leaves and grains.
- **Cause:** often linked to poor soil fertility and stress.
- **Actions:** correct nutrient deficiencies (potassium, silicon, zinc); use healthy seed.

## Brown planthopper (BPH)

- **Signs:** hoppers at the base of plants; yellowing and drying in patches ("hopperburn").
- **Actions:**
  1. Check the base of hills by tapping plants over water or a tray.
  2. Consider action only if numbers are high — roughly **10 or more hoppers per hill** in young crops (thresholds vary by country).
  3. **Drain** the field for 3–4 days and make alleys (about 30 cm walkways every 2–3 m) to let air and light reach the base.
  4. Avoid spraying pyrethroids and broad-spectrum insecticides early in the season; they kill natural enemies (spiders, mirid bugs) and can cause hopper outbreaks.
  5. Use a recommended selective insecticide only when thresholds are exceeded.

## Rats

After floods, rats move from flooded land to bunds, homesteads and surviving crops.
1. Organise **community rat control** at the same time across the whole area; individual efforts fail.
2. Keep bunds narrow (about 30 cm) and weed-free so rats cannot nest.
3. Use traps, burrow digging and smoking at the start of the season.
4. Use a trap barrier system (plastic fence with traps around an early-planted trap crop) where promoted.
5. Use rodenticide baits only as a community campaign and following label and safety instructions, away from children and animals.

## Golden apple snail (Philippines, Vietnam, Indonesia)

1. Handpick snails and pink egg masses in the morning and evening.
2. Put screens on water inlets.
3. Keep water shallow (about 2 cm) for the first 2 weeks after transplanting; **transplant** older seedlings (about 21–25 days) that snails damage less.
4. Let ducks into fields after harvest and before planting.

## Weeds

Floods and replanting leave gaps where weeds establish. Hand weed or use a rotary weeder about 15–20 and 30–40 days after transplanting.
